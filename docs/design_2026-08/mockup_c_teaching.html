Shelving is not cheating. Students often think suppressing an alarm is hiding a problem. Industry practice is the opposite: an unmanaged alarm list becomes noise, and noise is what operators learn to ignore. Shelving is bounded, timed, logged and attributed — the accountability is the discipline.

Why this console looks like this

A design-rationale page, in the product. Students meet display standards here as well as in the lectures.

Why is it mostly grey?

ISA-101 and the high-performance HMI movement reserve colour for abnormal conditions. A grey screen means a healthy spacecraft. Consoles that colour every subsystem look impressive and read slowly, because there is no colour left to signal with. Press Greyscale test in the header: this console remains fully operable with all colour removed, because every status also carries a glyph (▲ ▼ ⌀ ◇) and a border.

Why are the limits printed?

Limit checking is the core function of a mission control system. Values are compared against yellow and red bands defined in configs/eosat1/mcs/limits.yaml. Showing the numbers turns a colour reflex into an engineering judgement, and lets you argue about whether a limit is set correctly — which is itself an operations task.

Why four alarm priorities?

ISA-18.2 and EEMUA 191 assign priority by consequence × required response time: P1 under a minute, P2 minutes, P3 tens of minutes, P4 end of shift. Packet severity is a property of the message; priority is a property of what you must do about it. They are not the same thing, and conflating them is the most common alarm-system design error.

Why no blinking?

Only an unacknowledged P1 may blink, and it stops the moment you acknowledge it. Persistent animation on known conditions trains operators to ignore movement — the opposite of what it is for. Urgency here is carried by sort order, priority badge and the weight of the left edge.

Why is the future on screen?

Endsley's three levels of situation awareness are perception, comprehension and projection. Most telemetry displays stop at the first. Flight controllers are judged on the third — anticipating the eclipse, the buffer, the trip. Every subsystem screen here answers at least one question about the next thirty minutes.

Why is commanding on the telemetry page?

Because the point of the exercise is the loop: you command, the spacecraft changes, the telemetry moves, a limit is crossed, an alarm fires, a procedure responds. Splitting that loop across tabs hides the causality that the module exists to teach.

Mockup C — Teaching console. Causal chain as the organising spine · limits shown numerically and to scale · ISA-18.2 rationalisation text on every alarm · guided annotations that can be switched off for assessment. Honest trade-off: the annotation layer and rationale text cost screen space that a professional console would spend on parameters, and an experienced operator would find them patronising — which is exactly why guided mode is a toggle, and why an assessed run turns it off.
